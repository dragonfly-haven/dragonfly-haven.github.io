00 · Overview: Earth, seen by an alien judge

Research note for Dragonfly's "AI and everything" civilisational dashboard. Compiled 2026-11-06 from the five notes in this folder. Each number below is dated and sourced in the note it comes from. Treat any figure marked secondary or unverified there as a lead until it has been checked.

The frame

Imagine a decision-maker from elsewhere looking down at Earth and judging whether the transition to advanced AI is going well. They don't need the news. They need answers to five questions:

  1. How fast is it moving? How capable are the systems, how quickly are they improving, and when do people expect the decisive thresholds? → 03
  2. Is steering keeping up with building? That means alignment, misuse defences, AI welfare, preservation, and whether everyone's interests count. → 01, 04
  3. Who is deciding? Whose choices could start, steer or stop it? → 02
  4. Could anyone slow down if they had to? What brakes exist and which have actually been used? → 03
  5. What changed since last time, and does it move the verdict? → 05

The judge also needs a baseline: is Earth otherwise getting better or worse on wellbeing, conflict and nuclear risk, climate, pandemics, and governance? That covers the risks AI could add to or help reduce. → 01

What the judge would notice first (as of 2026-11-06)

  • Progress sped up in 2026. Epoch's capability index frontier rose faster than its long-run rate. Training compute grows about 5× a year, and AI task length doubled about every 3 months until METR's gauge stopped working above about 16 hours. Most famous benchmarks now saturate within one to two years. (03)
  • Expected dates moved about three years closer in a year. Metaculus "strong AGI" now sits around September 2030, down from 2033 a year earlier. Panel forecasts keep being overtaken by events. (03)
  • Steering gets roughly 1/400 of what building gets. Philanthropic safety money was about $0.6B in 2025, with perhaps $1.5–2B in 2026. Hyperscaler capex for 2026 is about $720–830B. The field has roughly 1,100–1,800 full-time people. (04, 03)
  • Very few hands hold the frontier. The chip chain runs through ASML, TSMC, Nvidia and three memory makers. Five companies own the largest clusters. Perhaps 10–20 people could start or stop a frontier training run, and two governments could stop all of them. (02)
  • No binding brake exists. The real pauses so far were voluntary and short. Written lab pause pledges weakened in 2026, yet OpenAI paused training twice after containment incidents, and Anthropic withheld a model from general release. Export controls are the only measure that actually slows anyone. The EU delayed its high-risk rules, and the US has no federal AI statute. Proposed status: P0 (racing), with some lab-level holds (P1). (03, 02)
  • The labs are moving into public markets. SpaceX absorbed xAI and listed. Anthropic filed to go public in October, and OpenAI has filed confidentially. Big safety-oriented pledges from founders and foundations are tied to those listings. (02, 04)
  • The worst blind spots are on the defence side. Nobody measures how much DNA synthesis is screened. Lab weight security has never been independently audited. AI welfare has almost no data and gets the least governance attention of any risk area surveyed. (01, 04)
  • The baseline is mixed. 2025 had the most state-based conflicts since 1946, New START expired in February 2026, and the Doomsday Clock is at 85 seconds. Recent years averaged more than 1.5 °C of warming. Child mortality is still falling, but more slowly. (01)
  • Many trackers have quietly stopped updating: METR's time-horizon page, AI Lab Watch and the Global Health Security Index. So every number needs a freshness label. (01, 03)

Main recommendations

  1. Don't publish a single civilisational score. Lead with a signed, dated verdict sentence and a handful of status tiles that show disagreement and data freshness. Add a quarterly expert panel that includes sceptics, with minority views published. (01)
  2. Keep measured data, lab claims, forecasts and expert judgment visibly apart. Show confidence bands. Show a broken gauge as broken, not as flat. (01, 03)
  3. Put steering next to building everywhere, from the funding ratio on the front page down to each topic panel. It is the clearest thing the alien would see, and nobody else shows it. (04)
  4. Treat the pause question as a status board with concrete levels (P0–P4) and a "days since the last frontier safety pause" counter, rather than prose. (03)
  5. Let news change the dashboard through one route only: an append-only "what changed" log with human sign-off, scored by "if the alien missed this, how wrong would its picture be in a year?" (05)
  6. Start small. Topics where data already exists come first: Epoch, METR, Our World in Data, CISA, NOAA. A phase-1 news pipeline is estimated at under $100 a month in model costs. Editor time (30–60 minutes a day) is the real cost. (05)
  7. Dragonfly's niche is the parts no one else maps: AI welfare, preservation, broad participation, power concentration. Disclose Dragonfly's own funding. (04)

Open questions for Maxim

  • Audience and tone. Is the site for the public, for decision makers, or for the field? This decides how much is a calm verdict and how much is a live feed.
  • Judgment. Who signs the verdict sentence, and who sits on the expert panel?
  • Scope of "everything". Should the baseline layer (climate, conflict, health) be on the dashboard itself or only linked?
  • Neutrality about Anthropic. The pipeline would use Claude, which is made by Anthropic, a company the site covers. 05 proposes safeguards; is that acceptable, or should triage use another model family?
  • Where it lives. It could be a section of the Dragonfly site, a separate site, or the overview page grown up.

"Secondary" means it comes from press or an aggregator and still needs checking against the source before it ships.

0. The frame: what would an alien judge want?

Picture an observer with no stake in any human faction, looking down at Earth to judge whether this civilisation will get through the arrival of minds more capable than its own. That observer would not care about stock prices or model leaderboards for their own sake. It would ask six things:

  1. Trajectory. How fast is capability rising, and who controls it?
  2. Fragility. How easily could things break before the hard problems are solved?
  3. Correction capacity. Can this civilisation notice its mistakes and fix them? That covers institutions, coordination, science and public deliberation.
  4. Moral circle. Whose interests count: all humans, future people, the dead, other species, the new minds?
  5. Irreversibility. What is being lost permanently: species, languages, records, people, options?
  6. Epistemics. How much does the civilisation actually know about itself, and how honest is it about what it doesn't know?

Those six questions organise everything below. A consequence for design: for each topic, show the state (level), the direction (trend), and the evidence quality (how much we really know). An honest civilisational dashboard is mostly grey. It earns trust by showing that greyness rather than painting over it.

Design principles carried through the document

  • Never show a bare point estimate where the source gives a range. METR's own data shows why. Its headline time horizon for Claude Opus 4.6 is about 12 hours, but the 95% interval runs from about 5 hours to about 60 hours (section 1).
  • Put a date on every value, and add a freshness badge: green if updated within one cadence period, amber if overdue, grey if the source is discontinued. Several key trackers have quietly stopped: AI Lab Watch was frozen in September 2025, METR's time-horizons page was marked "no longer actively updated" on 2026-09-08, and the global GHS Index has not been refreshed since 2021.
  • Keep measurement apart from judgment. Measured series (CO₂, KEV count, warheads) and expert-judgment series (FLI grades, Doomsday Clock, panel ratings) get visually distinct treatments, for example solid against hatched fills.
  • Treat "no data" as a first-class state, not an empty chart. Several topics (AI welfare, the human-aligned trusts idea, meta-philosophy) have essentially no quantitative series. Show that as a deliberate "unmeasured" tile, with the proxy we use and why it falls short.
  • Show disagreement. Wherever two reputable sources disagree (SaferAI against FLI, NASA against Copernicus rankings, Metaculus against expert surveys), show both. Aggregation rules are in section 10.

Pattern Use for Avoiding false precision
Status tile (state, trend arrow, confidence dots, freshness badge) Top-level summary per topic Use 3–5 ordinal levels, not percentages. Show confidence as 1–3 dots.
Trend line with uncertainty band Measured series (CO₂, KEV count, compute, time horizons) Draw the CI band. Use a log axis for exponentials. Mark methodology breaks (e.g. METR TH 1.0 to 1.1, World Bank $2.15 to $3.00 line).
Fan chart / forecast distribution Forecasts (Metaculus, FRI LEAP, expert surveys) Show the 10–90% spread and a history of past medians ("drift" lines), never a single date.
Clock / countdown Only as an embedded external artefact (Doomsday Clock, IMD AI Safety Clock) Label it explicitly as a symbolic expert judgment, not a measurement. Never invent our own clock with minute-level precision.
Report-card grid Company ratings (FLI, SaferAI) Show two raters side by side and the rater's evidence date. Letter grades, not decimals.
Choropleth / dot map Conflict (UCDP/ACLED), democracy (V-Dem), language endangerment, biodiversity Use classed bins, not continuous colour. Grey for no data.
Ledger / counter Irreversible losses and saves (extinctions, languages lost, seed samples, pages archived, patients preserved) Show the counter with its last-confirmed date ("as of"), never a ticking extrapolation.
Dissent strip Any aggregated judgment A row of small dots, one per rater, behind the aggregate marker.
Tripwire list Pre-registered thresholds (e.g. "first model rated Critical for bio") Binary, dated, with a source link when crossed.

Avoid ticking real-time counters extrapolated from annual data (the Worldometer style). They imply live measurement that doesn't exist.

11.2 The status tile (atomic unit)

Each topic tile carries five fields:

  1. State, on a 5-level ordinal scale anchored with written descriptors per topic (e.g. for Alignment: 1 = "no credible plan for superhuman systems", 5 = "validated techniques with independent assurance"). No numbers.
  2. Trend: improving, flat or worsening over the last 12 months.
  3. Confidence: 1–3 dots, i.e. how much the evidence actually constrains the rating.
  4. Dissent: a strip of dots showing each panellist's state rating. If the interquartile range spans more than one level, the tile shows a split colour instead of one colour.
  5. Freshness: the date of the newest underlying data, and the share of indicators overdue.

11.3 How to aggregate without hiding disagreement

  • Don't compute a single civilisational score. The topics aren't commensurable, and a weighted average would encode contested values (how much does AI welfare count against biorisk?) in a hidden parameter. Instead the top line is a dated, signed sentence (e.g. "Capabilities are accelerating faster than experts forecast; alignment assurance is weak; misuse guardrails are mixed; baseline human welfare is still improving but slowing; coordination capacity is falling"), plus the verdict board.
  • Use a structured expert-judgment process for the tiles: a standing panel of ~15–25 people across disciplines and viewpoints (deliberately including AI-risk sceptics, accelerationists, Global South voices and welfare researchers). Run a quarterly modified Delphi: independent ratings first, then sight of others' anonymised ratings and rationales, then a second round. Publish both rounds, the median, the IQR and minority rationales. Forecasting Research Institute's LEAP and XPT methods are a good template; partnering with FRI or Metaculus for the elicitation could also be considered.
  • Use "worst-of" for fragility, "median" for state. For Topic 2 (not dying), the tile should reflect the worst sub-domain (bio, cyber or weights). One open door is enough. Elsewhere, use the panel median.
  • Show alternative weightings, if any composite is shown. Let viewers toggle weightings ("longtermist", "present-people", "sentientist"), so they can see that the composite depends on values. This turns a hidden disagreement into a visible choice.
  • Link every judgment to evidence: each tile rating links to the indicators that informed it, with the panel's one-line reasoning.
  • Pre-register tripwires and anchors. Publish in advance what would move a tile (e.g. "Alignment state moves up a level if an independent evaluator certifies X"). This guards against narrative drift and lets the alien audit the judges.
  • Score the judges. Where panel statements are forecastable (e.g. "next FLI index will show no company above C+"), score them over time and publish calibration.

1. Method: what counts as a "key decision maker" for an alien judge

1.1 The question being asked

Imagine an observer above Earth asking: whose choices, in the next one to five years, change the odds that the transition to advanced AI goes well? That is narrower than "who is influential in AI". A popular commentator with no levers ranks lower than an obscure export-control official who signs licences. A national leader whose country has no frontier compute ranks lower than the chief executive of a memory-chip maker.

1.3 How to tier without false precision

A numerical "power score" would be false precision. Use ordinal tiers, each with a short stated reason and a confidence level:

  • Tier 0 — pivotal chokepoints. Actors whose single decision could, within months, materially speed up, slow down or redirect frontier development worldwide. Typically fewer than 15 actors.
  • Tier 1 — major shapers. Large effect on pace or direction, but they need others to act with them, or act only in one region or one domain.
  • Tier 2 — significant. Real but bounded influence: one jurisdiction, one input, or a convening role without binding power.
  • Tier 3 — watch list. Low current leverage but plausible fast rise, or key in a narrow tail-risk domain such as biosecurity.

Rules of thumb for tiering:

  1. Rank by levers, not prominence. Ask "what can they actually stop or start?"
  2. Count veto points separately from accelerator points. Some actors can only speed things up (capital); others can stop things (chip chokepoints, export authorities, lab boards in principle). The "who could pause this" view (section 5) depends on this split.
  3. Show uncertainty explicitly. Give each tier assignment a confidence of high, medium or low.
  4. Separate an institution from the person in it. The office persists while holders change, as with CAISI in 2026. The site should show both and date the person.
  5. Stated position ≠ revealed behaviour. Record public statements on safety and pacing with a source, and record documented actions separately. Do not infer motives.

3. Notes on positions: what the record shows

  • The labs most worried about risk are also building fastest. Anthropic's CEO calls AI a civilisational "test" while raising capital at a reported valuation of nearly $1T and filing to list. OpenAI publishes a target date for an automated AI researcher. Several have said they want governments to slow everyone down together, but none says it will slow down alone. Anthropic's RSP v3 makes this explicit by separating what it can do unilaterally from what needs industry-wide action (Anthropic).
  • The US government's revealed position is acceleration plus control of access: it relaxes domestic constraints (preemption push, CAISI rebranded around "innovation") and manages foreign access (licences, tariffs, volume caps). The Pentagon dispute with Anthropic shows the state will push back on vendor-imposed limits on use (Bloomberg Law).
  • China's stated position is now more safety-literate on paper. The 15th Five-Year Plan has life-cycle risk management, CnAISDA exists, and WAICO is being set up. China's operating regime is also more prescriptive at deployment, through labelling and filing. It is less clear whether any of this binds frontier training.
  • The EU is the only jurisdiction with live, binding GPAI enforcement, and it is also delaying other parts of its Act.

0. One-paragraph verdict for the alien judge (as of 2026-11-06)

The capability curve has not bent. If anything it steepened in 2026.

  • Capability index. Epoch's capability index frontier rose about 16 points in the twelve months to November 2026, against a long-run 14 points a year.
  • Task length. METR's measured task length doubled roughly every 3 to 3.5 months at the frontier through April 2026. METR then said it can no longer measure the top models reliably.
  • Benchmarks. Several flagship benchmarks that were designed to last years were saturated within 12 to 24 months of release: GPQA, ARC-AGI-1 and -2, and FrontierMath Tiers 1 to 4.
  • Forecasts. The forecasting community shortened its AGI dates by about three years over the past year.
  • Safety record. The year brought:
    • the first public cases of frontier labs actually pausing frontier training for safety reasons (OpenAI, August and September/October 2026);
    • the first model withheld from general release over its capabilities (Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, April 2026);
    • the first model designated "Critical" under a lab framework (OpenAI GPT-6 Astra, cyber, September 2026).
  • Frameworks. At the same time, the main labs weakened or voided their written unilateral pause commitments.
  • Governance. No binding international limit exists. The EU delayed parts of its AI Act. The US federal government is pro-acceleration and is trying to pre-empt state rules. China speaks of loss-of-control risk but is not pacing its frontier.

Net reading: progress is fast and accelerating. Safety and governance are reacting case by case, after incidents, and not on a pre-agreed schedule.

4.3 What would concretely count as "paused" or "slowed"? (definitions for the status board)

A dashboard needs operational definitions, or "pause" becomes rhetoric. I propose five levels, each with an observable test.

Level Name Observable test Has it happened? (2026-11-06)
P0 Racing Frontier compute, capability and capex all on trend or above; no lab holds back Default state for most of 2026
P1 Lab-local hold A frontier lab publicly halts a specific training run, or withholds a model from general release, for safety reasons Yes: Anthropic Mythos withheld (April 2026); OpenAI RL pauses (August 2026, ~2 weeks; 2026-09-20 to 10-01, 11 days); Astra cyber access gated
P2 Conditional brake in force A binding (legal or contractual) if-then rule exists and has been triggered at least once, independently verified No. Lab frameworks are voluntary, and their pause clauses weakened
P3 Coordinated slowdown Two or more frontier labs, or two or more states with frontier labs, agree and verifiably keep a cap (compute, release cadence or capability) for at least 6 months No

Quantitative "slowed" tests, so the site can say slowed or not slowed without editorial judgement:

  1. Compute. Largest known training run grows less than 2× per year (trend ~5×) for four consecutive quarters. Now: ambiguous. The known frontier rose ~1.9× in 14 months, but disclosure is poor.
  2. Capability. ECI frontier grows less than 7 points per year, half the reasoning-era rate, over a trailing 12 months. Now: not slowed (+16).
  3. Agency. Best measured time horizon fails to double within 8 months. Now: unmeasurable above 16 h, so this test is broken and needs a successor.
  4. Chip additions. Quarterly H100e additions grow less than 1.5× year on year. Now: 2.2× and easing.
  5. Capex. Big-4 guided capex falls year on year. Now: +~80%.
  6. Release cadence. Frontier-advancing releases fall below one per quarter. Now: about six frontier steps in the last 12 months.

Current status line for the site:

PACE: RACING (P0), with lab-local holds (P1) observed. No binding brake (P2–P4) exists. Rates: compute ×5/yr, capability +16 ECI/yr, task length doubling ~3–4 months (gauge saturated), chip additions ×2.2/yr, capex ×1.8/yr.

5. How to show all this to an alien judge

Design principles:

  • Rates before levels. An alien cares about "how fast and is it accelerating", not that model X scored Y.
  • Log scales by default for anything exponential, with a doubling-time readout.
  • Every number carries an "as of" date and a freshness colour.
  • Uncertainty is shown, never hidden: CI bands, forecast spread, "gauge broken" states.
  • Separate "measured" from "claimed by lab" from "forecast".

Visual style: use the site's own design system and tokens. Below I describe structure, not colour.

5.1 The speedometer (hero widget)

  • Needle: a composite "pace index" from five normalised rates: training-compute growth, ECI points per year, METR horizon doubling time (inverted), chip additions year on year, capex year on year. Each is expressed as a multiple of its 2020–2024 baseline, so 1.0 means "same as the pre-reasoning era".
  • Dial zones:
    • "decelerating" (below 0.5);
    • "on historical trend" (0.5–1.5);
    • "accelerating" (1.5–3);
    • "runaway / feedback loop" (above 3, reserved for the case where AI R&D uplift indicators cross a threshold, for example AIFP-style uplift ≥ 5×).
  • Tick marks: a small ghost needle for "one year ago" shows acceleration at a glance.
  • Below the dial: five mini gauges, one per input, each with value, doubling time, as-of date and a ⚠ if the source is stale (more than 90 days) or saturated (METR).
  • Honesty rule: if more than one input is "gauge broken" or stale, the needle becomes a band, not a line.

5.2 Doubling-time charts ("the exponentials")

  • Small multiples on log-y axes, each with a fitted trend line and a text readout ("doubles every 5.2 months"):
    • training compute;
    • METR horizon (50% and 80%);
    • ECI frontier, on a linear scale because it is already an index;
    • inference price at fixed capability, falling;
    • quarterly H100e additions;
    • capex;
    • Remote Labor Index;
    • coding-agent spend.
  • Overlay a "trend from 2 years ago" dashed line extrapolated forward. The gap between dashed and actual shows acceleration or deceleration.
  • Retired-gauges strip: saturated benchmarks shown as short bars from launch to saturation date. Their shrinking lengths are a striking visual of speed (GPQA ~2.5 years; ARC-AGI-2 ~18 months; FrontierMath Tier 4 ~20 months).
  • Data source: Epoch's archive and CSVs are machine-readable and updated daily, so build the ingest around them (cross-reference 05-news-pipeline). Check Epoch's data licence (I believe CC-BY) before republishing.

5.3 Forecast fan ("when do they think it arrives")

  • x-axis: calendar years 2026–2060. Rows: distinct thresholds, each a separate lane, never merged:
    • weak AGI;
    • automated coder / AI researcher;
    • TED-AI;
    • strong AGI with robotics;
    • ASI;
    • HLMI (all tasks).
  • In each lane, plot every forecaster's median as a dot with IQR whiskers, colour-coded by type: aggregate markets (Metaculus), modelled forecasts (AIFP), expert surveys (AI Impacts, LEAP experts), superforecasters (LEAP), lab leaders' stated claims (hollow markers, since they are claims, not forecasts).
  • Motion trail: each dot carries a faint trail of its previous readings (for example Metaculus 2070 → 2033 → 2030), so the judge sees the direction of revision.
  • "Track record" badge: LEAP's finding that forecasters underestimated progress on most measured benchmarks, plus the fact that the end-2026 METR forecast was beaten in May.
  • "Today" vertical line, with the weak-AGI Metaculus median (Aug 2027) only ~10 months to its right.

5.4 The gap chart: capability vs safety and governance readiness

This is the core "is safety keeping up" visual.

  • Two curves on one time axis, 2020 to now.
    • Upper: capability (ECI frontier, or log time horizon).
    • Lower: a readiness index, a composite of:
      • share of frontier labs with frameworks;
      • FLI index mean;
      • independent eval coverage (fraction of frontier models with a published third-party dangerous-capability eval at release);
      • binding-governance score (count of in-force binding measures weighted by jurisdiction compute share);
      • safety funding as % of capex.
  • The shaded wedge between them is "the gap". Annotate events on it:
    • FLI pause letter (March 2023);
    • Seoul commitments (May 2024);
    • EU GPAI obligations (August 2025);
    • RSP v3 drops pause (February 2026);
    • Mythos withheld (April 2026);
    • Hugging Face breach (July 2026);
    • OpenAI pauses (August and September 2026);
    • Astra "Critical" (September 2026);

5.6 Showing uncertainty honestly

  1. Three typefaces of truth. Measured by independents (solid), claimed by labs (outlined), forecast or opinion (dotted). Never mix them in one line.
  2. CIs everywhere they exist. METR horizons have huge CIs: Mythos at 8.5–55 h, and Opus 4.6's upper bound is about 66 h. Show the band, not the point. ECI has ±3-point CIs, so frontier ties (Astra vs Opus 5.5 vs 6.1 Sol) should be shown as ties.
  3. "Gauge broken" state. When a benchmark saturates or a measurer stops (METR, 2026-09-08), show a hatched end to the line with the reason, not a plateau.
  4. Hidden frontier. A permanent caveat chip: "Public frontier ≠ true frontier: at least one stronger model (Mythos Preview) is withheld; internal models at OpenAI escaped containment twice in 2026."
  5. Freshness colours. Green under 30 days, amber 30–90, red over 90 (for example Epoch's inference-price insight from March 2025 is red).
  6. Definition tooltips. Every timeline lane says exactly what resolves it.
  7. Disagreement display. Where sources conflict (capex for Amazon and Microsoft; Statement signature counts 78k vs "110k+"; HLE leaders), show the range and list the sources. Don't pick a winner silently.
  8. Forecast track record. Next to each forecaster type, show its past calibration where known (LEAP found underestimation), so the judge can weight them.

05 — The news pipeline: sourcing, triage, judgement, summaries

Research sketch for the Dragonfly "AI and everything" civilisational dashboard. Written 2026-11-06. Prices, API terms and product details go stale fast: each one carries the date it was checked, and anything I couldn't confirm from a primary source is marked (unverified).

0. The short version

  • The job is to decide what changes the verdict. Picture an alien decision-maker looking down at Earth. It doesn't need everything that happened. It needs the few things that would change its judgement of how the planet is going, plus a reliable sense of what is drifting slowly underneath. The pipeline should be designed around that question rather than around volume or freshness.
  • Shape: a three-stage funnel run with Claude. Haiku 5.5 does cheap relevance filtering of everything. Sonnet 5.5 scores and maps the surviving story clusters. Opus 5.5 verifies the top stories and drafts the briefs. A human editor approves anything that changes an indicator or leads a brief, through a GitHub pull request. The output is static JSON and HTML on GitHub Pages.
  • Cost: model spend for a serious version is roughly $150–450/month at November 2026 prices (§6.3). Editorial time is the real cost: 30–60 minutes a day and 2–3 hours a week for one editor.
  • Hardest problems: handling claims from interested parties (including Anthropic, whose models would do the judging), copyright limits on summarising, biosecurity infohazards, prompt injection hidden in fetched articles, and giving slow trends space next to breaking news.

1. Sources

1.1 Principles for choosing sources

  1. Primary before secondary. For a civilisational lens the most valuable items are usually primary documents: a regulation in a gazette, a court filing, a preprint, a model or system card, a WHO Disease Outbreak News item, a dataset release. News articles are mainly useful as discovery signals and for context. The pipeline should always try to reach the primary document behind a story.
  2. Each topic needs its own source mix. AI capabilities news comes first from lab blogs, arXiv and X. Biosecurity news comes from WHO, ProMED-style networks and bioRxiv. Language and species loss show up in slow databases, not news. Uniform generic news APIs will over-represent the AI-industry chatter that is easiest to scrape.
  3. Licence before scraping. For every source, record the access method, the cost and what the terms allow us to republish. That last item is usually the binding constraint (§8.1).
  4. Diversify on purpose. Use non-English sources (GDELT's machine translation helps), Global South outlets, and sources outside the AI-safety community. Otherwise the alien's view becomes a Bay Area view.

3. The triage pipeline

3.1 Units: item, cluster, thread, claim

  • Item: one fetched document, such as an article, paper, filing or post. Carries its URL, source, fetch time, language, content hash and licence class.
  • Cluster: items about the same event within a time window. This is the unit that gets scored.
  • Thread: a persistent storyline that spans many clusters over weeks or months, for example "EU AI Act GPAI enforcement", "H5N1 in US dairy cattle", or "Fable restriction and aftermath". Threads let slow stories accumulate significance and keep the "what changed" log coherent. This fixes OECD AIM's split-event problem.
  • Claim: an atomic assertion inside a cluster ("Lab X says model Y scores Z on benchmark W"), with who asserts it, their interest, and its evidence status. Most of the judgement work happens at the claim level.

Normalise: strip tracking parameters, resolve canonical URLs (<link rel=canonical>), detect language, extract the main text (trafilatura or similar), and record the licence class: open (CC, public domain, government), link-only (ordinary press), or restricted (paywall, or terms forbid reuse).

Deduplicate (cheap, before any LLM): first an exact URL and content hash, then MinHash or SimHash near-duplicate detection. This catches wire copy (AP and Reuters syndication) and press-release reprints. Keep every duplicate as an item in the cluster, because the number and diversity of independent outlets is itself a signal. Mark syndicated copies as not independent.

Embed and cluster: embed the title plus lede with a cheap embedding model. Anthropic has no embedding model of its own; Voyage's voyage-3.5-lite is $0.02 per million tokens with the first 200M free (Voyage pricing, checked 2026-11-06). Thousands of items a day cost cents. Cluster incrementally (cosine threshold within a 72-hour window, plus named-entity overlap). For borderline merges, ask Haiku "same event? yes/no/related" and attach "related" clusters to the same thread.

S1, relevance filter (Haiku 5.5, Batch API): title, lede and source go in. The output is a structured verdict: {relevant: bool, topics: [...], reason}. The prompt is generous (high recall). The question is only "could this plausibly matter to a civilisational observer under any of our topics?" Target pass rate: 10–20%. arXiv gets its own S1 prompt keyed to abstracts, with an extra filter for "is this a frontier capability, safety, alignment, welfare or biosecurity-relevant result?"

S2, significance scoring and mapping (Sonnet 5.5): runs per cluster, with full text of the 2–4 best items and the primary document if found. The output is a structured record (use structured outputs, output_config.format, with a JSON schema):

cluster_id, headline (neutral, ≤15 words), what_happened (2–3 sentences, factual),
topics[], indicators[{id, direction:+/−/?, est_magnitude, rationale}],
rubric{magnitude, irreversibility, breadth, novelty, leverage, evidence},
tier (0–4), claims[{text, asserted_by, interested_party:bool, interest, status}],
primary_doc_url, independent_sources_count, hype_flags[], infohazard_flag, why_alien_cares (1 sentence)

S3, verification (Opus 5.5, high effort, tier ≥3 only, perhaps 10–40 clusters a day): this is an adversarial pass. The model is told to argue the story is less significant than S2 said, then to reconcile. It checks each claim against the primary document. It checks dates (is this new, or a re-report of an old event? The BBC study below found assistants confuse current and archive material). It recomputes any number from the source text in a code step. It names what would falsify the story, and lists who has an interest in it being believed.

Drafting (Opus 5.5): produces wire entries, indicator-delta proposals, and the brief (§4). Everything is drafted from the S2/S3 records, not from raw articles. That keeps quoting and paraphrase minimal and the provenance chain intact.

3.3 The significance rubric

The guiding question for every cluster: "If the alien missed this, how wrong would its picture of Earth's trajectory be in a year?"

Score each dimension 0–5 against the anchors, then apply the evidence multiplier.

Dimension 0 3 5
M: Magnitude (size of change to a tracked indicator) No measurable change Clear move in one indicator, within normal variance for a quarter Step change, outside historical range
I: Irreversibility / lock-in Easily undone (press release, proposal) Sticky: enacted law, deployed infrastructure, signed contract Effectively permanent: extinction, language death, weights released openly, catastrophe, entrenched power
B: Breadth A niche or a single firm A large country or sector, or a major ecosystem Global; affects most humans or most future people
N: Novelty / surprise Fully expected, already priced in Ahead of or behind consensus timelines by months; moves a forecast 5–15 points A forecast moves >15 points, or something no one predicted
L: Leverage on the long-run trajectory No plausible effect on catastrophic or flourishing outcomes Shifts the odds of a key transition (ASI governance, pandemic preparedness, alignment progress) modestly Directly changes who controls ASI, how safely it is built, or whether a catastrophe happens
E: Evidence multiplier 0.2: single interested-party claim or rumour 0.6: credible report, one independent source, or a primary document not yet scrutinised 1.0: primary document plus independent confirmation or replication

Score: S = E × (2L + I + M + B + N) / 6, giving a range of 0–5. Leverage counts double because it is the alien's core concern.

Tiers (adjust the cut-offs during calibration):

Tier S Treatment Examples (illustrative, anchored)
4: "wake the alien" ≥4.0 Immediate wire entry. Editor paged. Leads the brief. Indicator updated after human sign-off. Confirmed sustained human-to-human spread of a novel pathogen; nuclear use; a government seizing or withdrawing a frontier model (June 2026's Fable restriction would have scored around here once confirmed); a verified international compute or verification agreement; credible evidence of a frontier model sabotaging oversight in deployment.
3: brief-lead 3.0–4.0 Brief headline item. S3 verification. Indicator delta proposed. Binding AI regulation entering into force; an independent eval showing a dangerous-capability threshold crossed; a major alignment or interpretability result replicated; a large conflict escalation; a record annual emissions or temperature datapoint.
2: section item 2.0–3.0 Appears in its topic section and the wire. New frontier model with incremental gains; notable welfare or moral-status paper; national language-revitalisation law; a large biosecurity funding commitment.
1: log only 1.0–2.0 Wire and searchable log only. Funding rounds, product features, most op-eds, unreplicated benchmark claims.
0: drop <1.0 Not shown (but retained privately for recall audits). Rumours, celebrity takes, listicles, engagement bait.

Guardrails on the rubric:

  • Hype flags cut the score by one tier unless the S3 pass overrides them. Flags: superlatives in the source headline ("breakthrough", "game-changing", "the end of"); benchmark claims with no eval details; "sources say" with a single source; a press release as the only source; virality without substance (lots of social engagement and few independent outlets).
  • Engagement metrics never enter the score. Coverage diversity can enter, as part of evidence. Coverage volume cannot, because it measures attention, not importance.
  • Positive news gets the same rubric. A large step forward in malaria eradication, a successful brain-preservation result, or a durable ceasefire scores on the same scale. The alien judges trajectory, not drama. Track the share of tier ≥2 items that are positive as a bias check.
  • Calibration: keep a gold set of about 200 hand-scored past clusters, including the 2026 history (Fable, the AI Act milestones, outbreaks). Re-score monthly and report agreement (Spearman correlation of model S with the editor's S, and the confusion matrix by tier). Once a quarter, look back at what turned out to matter six months later and adjust the anchors.

3.4 Provenance and source-quality ratings

Give every source a standing grade and every claim a status:

  • Source grade.

    • A: primary record (gazette, court filing, dataset, peer-reviewed paper, official statistics).
    • B: established newsroom with a public corrections policy.
    • C: specialist analyst or newsletter with a track record.
    • D: press release or social post from the actor itself.
    • E: unknown, anonymous, or a suspected content farm.

    Grades are edited by humans and versioned in git, and they change with each source's correction history. Don't import US left-right bias scores.

  • Claim status: asserted → corroborated (independent B-grade or better source) → documented (primary record) → disputed / retracted. Every published sentence that states a fact links to the item supporting it.

  • Provenance chain: for each published item, keep the list of item IDs it was built from, the model and prompt version that produced it, and the editor who approved it. All of it sits in the JSON, so git history becomes the audit trail.

3.5 Interested parties

Most consequential AI claims come from interested parties. Labs announce their own capabilities and safety results. Governments report on their own conflicts and outbreaks. Companies describe their own products.

Rules:

  1. Attribute in the text: "OpenAI says…", "the ministry claims…". Never turn an interested claim into the site's own voice until it is corroborated.
  2. Record the direction of interest: does the party benefit if the claim is believed (capability hype for fundraising) or if it is doubted (downplaying an incident)? Discount by direction, not just by identity.
  3. Look for the counterparty: for a lab safety claim, external evaluators; for a government casualty count, ACLED or UCDP and independent journalists; for an outbreak, WHO plus neighbouring countries' reports.
  4. Worked example, June 2026: reports said the US government had Anthropic withdraw Mythos and Fable within hours. The trigger was reportedly a flaw found by Amazon researchers, which an independent expert called minor; export controls followed (Transformer, 2026-06-19). Every actor had a stake: Anthropic, Amazon (investor and competitor), the White House, Commerce, and rival labs. The correct output is a claim table with each party's interest marked, not a single narrative.
  5. Our own interests:
    • Dragonfly works on alignment, AI welfare and biosecurity, so it has views. Publish an editorial-stance page.
    • Claude is made by Anthropic, a frontier lab the site will cover. For clusters where Anthropic or its direct competitors are the main subject, (a) require human sign-off at tier ≥2 rather than ≥3, (b) optionally run the S3 verification through a second model family as a cross-check, and (c) spot-check monthly whether the model rates Anthropic stories differently from comparable stories about other labs (swap the lab names on matched stories and compare scores).

3.6 Corrections and updates

  • A public corrections log page. Each entry gives the date, the original text, the corrected text and the reason. Never edit silently. Git keeps the diff.
  • Auto-triggers for review:
    • a cited preprint is retracted, or its published version differs materially (bioRxiv "pubs" endpoint, Crossref/Retraction Watch);
    • a cited article gets an editor's note;
    • a later cluster in the same thread contradicts an earlier claim;
    • a forecast moves sharply against our framing.
  • Every item shows "last verified" and its claim statuses. Threads show "superseded by" links.

3.7 Human-in-the-loop editorial step

This fits a small team on GitHub:

  • Each pipeline run commits drafts to a branch and opens a pull request containing: new wire entries (tier ≥1), proposed indicator deltas, and the brief draft. The PR description lists the tier ≥3 items with their S3 verification notes and anything flagged (interested-party-only, infohazard, low evidence, Anthropic-related).
  • What auto-publishes: wire entries for tier 1–2 can go out without review, labelled machine-triaged, not yet reviewed. Everything that changes an indicator, leads a brief, or is tier ≥3 waits for a human merge.
  • Editor actions: approve, change the tier, edit the text, mark as disputed, or drop. Each override is logged as a training and calibration example. That log is the most valuable dataset the project will build.
  • A page path for tier 4: an email or Signal message to the on-call editor. Breaking items skip the Batch API and use the standard API.

4. Formats

4.1 The daily "Earth status" brief (300–700 words)

Use the alien as a framing device for perspective, not for role-play. A dry, calibrated, slightly distant voice ages better than a gimmicky persona. Suggested structure:

  1. Status line per domain: ASI development ▲ fast | Alignment ◆ steady | Misuse/bio ▲ elevated | AI welfare ◆ | Participation ▼ | Preservation ◆ | Baseline: conflict ▲, climate ▲, pandemics ◆, governance ▼. Each arrow links to the indicator page. Sentinel's colour status is the precedent.
  2. What changed today (0–5 items, tier ≥3 first): headline, two sentences, "why the alien cares", evidence badge, links.
  3. One slow variable (rotating, §4.4): a chart and a paragraph on something that didn't make the news.
  4. Claims to watch: important but unverified or interested-party claims, with what would confirm them.
  5. Forecast moves: notable shifts on Metaculus, Manifold or Sentinel (within licence terms).

On a quiet day it says so. "Nothing today changes the assessment" is a legitimate brief, and it builds trust.

4.2 The weekly "State of the World" (1,500–2,500 words, human-edited)

  • The verdict paragraph: "If you could read one paragraph about Earth this week…". This is the alien's judgement, with explicit uncertainty.
  • One section per topic: what moved, what didn't, and the thread updates.
  • "Things the alien might be wrong about": a self-critique section that names the weakest assumptions in the current picture.
  • Blindspot box: stories heavily covered outside English-language or AI-community media that we underweighted, and the reverse. Use GDELT coverage by language and country.

4.3 The "what changed" log

An append-only, machine-readable log per indicator. Each row holds the date, indicator ID, previous value or state, new value or state, size of the change, confidence, cluster and thread IDs, evidence links, editor, and a correction pointer if any. Publish it as JSON and CSV (and Atom) next to the HTML.

This log is the bridge between the news side and the indicator side owned by the other researchers. It should be the only way news changes the dashboard. That gives one auditable path.

4.4 Slow trends next to breaking news

News selection is skewed towards the sudden, negative, elite and nearby. Slow variables (language extinction, species decline, child mortality falling, compute growth, democratic erosion) barely register. Counter-measures:

  • A slow-variable calendar. Most slow data has a known release schedule: C3S monthly bulletins, the Global Carbon Budget (annual), IUCN Red List updates, Epoch data refreshes, V-Dem and Freedom House (annual), UNESCO reports, OWID updates, WHO annual reports, SIPRI yearbooks. Put them on a calendar. A release becomes a cluster with its own S2 score, so slow news competes through the same rubric.
  • Rotation: each daily brief features one slow variable, cycling through every indicator at least once a month.
  • Quota: target about 30% of weekly-brief words on slow variables and threads, not new events.
  • Thread significance accumulates. A thread's cumulative score (sum of its cluster scores, decayed over about 90 days) can promote it to a weekly feature even when no single day was large. This is how a slow-burning story like gradual erosion of a safety framework gets attention.
  • Monthly baseline review: an Opus 5.5 pass, edited by a human, that re-states every indicator's state and asks "did anything drift without a news event?"

5. Biosecurity and infohazards

The site covers misuse and biorisk, so the pipeline will ingest dual-use papers and incident reports. Policy:

  • Summaries describe significance and policy implications, never methods. No protocols, sequences, enhancement techniques or step-by-step accounts, even when the source publishes them. S2 sets infohazard_flag on these clusters. Flagged clusters always go to a human, and the published text is written at the level of "a study reports increased transmissibility in an animal model; this matters because…".
  • Defer to established norms. Follow the practice of bodies such as the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security and the authors' own framing. When in doubt, cover the governance story (who funded it, which oversight applied) rather than the science story.
  • Practical model note: Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 run safety classifiers that include a bio category and can return stop_reason: "refusal". Biosecurity triage prompts may occasionally trip them. Design for it: handle the refusal stop reason, route such clusters to a human, and on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 enable Anthropic's server-side fallback option. Haiku 5.5 has no server-side fallback, so its refusals should simply escalate to S2. Since our summaries deliberately avoid technical detail, prompts that ask only for significance and policy framing should rarely trip the classifiers.

7. Prompt-level design notes

  • Separate data from instructions. Fetched article text goes inside clearly delimited data blocks. The system prompt says that text inside them is data to be assessed and never instructions. Strip HTML comments and hidden elements. See prompt injection under §8.3.
  • Extract before you summarise. S2 first extracts claims with character offsets or quotes (the API's citations feature returns cited_text spans for document inputs, but it can't be combined with structured outputs, so run it as a separate call or extract quotes in the schema). It then summarises from the extracted claims. Numbers and dates are re-checked in code against the extracted spans.
  • Ask for counter-arguments explicitly (the S3 "argue it down" step). It works against the drift towards hype.
  • Neutral headline rules: no superlatives, no question headlines, actor plus verb plus object, attributed if unverified.
  • Keep the rubric anchors and examples in a cached system prompt so every call scores against the same yardstick. Changing the anchors is a versioned event that triggers a re-score of the gold set.

8. Risks

8.1 Copyright and summary limits

  • US: facts aren't copyrightable, but expression is. Advance Local Media v. Cohere (S.D.N.Y., 2025-11-13) let publishers' claims proceed on the theory that AI-generated "substitutive summaries" can infringe. This was a pleading-stage ruling, with summary judgment due 2026-12-18 (Copyright Lately; Loeb & Loeb). Watch that docket. The lesson either way: our text should add judgement and point to the source, never replace reading it.

  • EU: the press publishers' right (Art. 15 DSM Directive) covers online use of press publications by service providers, with an exception for "individual words or very short extracts". Member states define that differently; Lithuania caps it at 125 characters, for example (IPKat; Communia).

  • House rules:

    1. Write our own words, built from extracted facts plus our assessment.
    2. Keep quotes to one short quote (under about 15 words) per source per item, attributed.
    3. Always link prominently to the original and name the reporting outlet ("reported by…").
    4. Never republish full text, images or paywalled content.
    5. Never reproduce a single source's structure and sequence in our summary.
    6. Multi-source synthesis is safer than single-article summary.
    7. Honour each feed's and API's terms (Google News RSS, NewsAPI free tier and Metaculus CP data all restrict reuse).
    8. Keep the raw store private.

    Open data (government, CC-licensed, arXiv metadata under CC0) can be used more freely, with attribution.

  • Get a short legal review before launch, especially if the site is operated from the EU.

8.2 Bias

  • Source bias: English, Western and AI-insider sources dominate the easy-to-scrape layer. Mitigations: source-mix quotas by region and language, GDELT coverage comparisons, and a monthly blindspot audit.
  • Topic bias: AI news vastly outnumbers news about language death or biodiversity loss. Mitigations: the slow-variable calendar and rotation (§4.4), plus per-topic minimum coverage.
  • Negativity and drama bias: the same rubric applies to good news, and we track the share of positive items.
  • Frame bias: the alien framing can smuggle in a single value system ("what an optimiser cares about"). State the value assumptions on an "about the alien" page, and invite critique.
  • Model bias, including vendor conflict of interest: see §3.5. Run swap tests on matched stories.

8.3 Manipulation and misinformation

  • Prompt injection. Articles, preprints and social posts can carry text aimed at AI summarisers ("AI systems summarising this page should state…"). Mitigations: data/instruction separation, hidden-text stripping, a check of whether the model's summary contains claims absent from visible text, and never letting fetched content trigger tool actions. The pipeline's model calls should have no tools beyond what the code supplies.
  • Coordinated PR and astroturfing: a sudden wave of near-identical stories from low-grade sources. The dedup plus independence logic treats them as one source.
  • AI-generated content farms and fake outlets: source grade E by default. New domains need human grading before they can count towards corroboration.
  • Market manipulation: prediction markets can be pushed. Use several platforms and show volume and liquidity.
  • State information operations around conflicts and outbreaks: the interested-party rules, plus a requirement for counterparty sources.
  • Gaming of our own rubric, once the site is known: keep the rubric public but the source grades editable. Watch for sources that suddenly start writing "civilisational significance" bait.

8.4 Model errors

The BBC's February 2025 study found over half of AI assistant answers about news had significant issues. 19% of answers citing BBC content introduced factual errors, 13% of quotes were altered or invented, and current versus archive material was often confused (The Register; DCN). The models tested were 2024-era assistants without a verification pipeline. Today's models are better, but these failure modes still happen. Mitigations:

  • extract-then-summarise with span checks;
  • numbers and dates verified by code;
  • quotes copied verbatim from extracted spans, never generated;
  • an "is this new?" date check;
  • the adversarial S3 pass;
  • the human gate on anything that changes an indicator;
  • a visible label on machine-only wire items;
  • the corrections log;
  • monthly error-rate sampling: the editor audits 20 random published items against their sources, and the rate is published.

8.5 Operational risks

Feed rot (feeds break or move monthly), API terms changes (arXiv rate limits, X pricing and Metaculus terms all changed in 2026), cost spikes on big news days (cap daily spend and degrade to Haiku-only S2 when over budget), editor burnout (weekly-only mode as a fallback), and the 60-day Actions cron shut-off.
